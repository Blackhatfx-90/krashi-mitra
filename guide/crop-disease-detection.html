How Krishi Mitra Detects Crop Disease from a Photo

Krishi Mitra (also written Krashi Mitra) · crop disease detection

A farmer notices a patch of yellowing leaves and wants to know what it is before spending money on medicine. In Krishi Mitra, that takes one photo. You pick your crop, take a picture of the affected leaf, and the app names the disease and tells you what to do about it — organically first, chemically if that is not enough.

What happens after you press the button

The photo never has to leave your phone. Krishi Mitra downloads a small AI model for each crop, and that model runs on the phone itself. This is why disease detection keeps working in a field with no signal, and why the scan does not use your data once the model is downloaded.

Three checks before it answers

An AI model that only knows ten wheat diseases will confidently name a wheat disease for a photo of a wall, a hand, or a sugarcane field. That is how these models are built — they always pick the closest label they know. Krishi Mitra therefore checks three things before it will answer at all:

  • Is this a plant? The app looks at the greenness, texture and edges in the photo. A photo of a face or a floor is refused instead of being given a disease name.
  • Is this the crop you selected? Grass-family crops like wheat, rice and sugarcane have long parallel-veined leaves; cotton, tomato and mango have broad ones. If you selected apple and photographed sugarcane, the app says so rather than inventing an answer.
  • Is the plant actually damaged? If the leaf looks healthy, Krishi Mitra says it is healthy and gives advice on keeping it that way. It does not hunt for a disease that is not there.

When you have internet

With a network available, Krishi Mitra can also send the photo for a second opinion from a larger online model and compare the two answers. This is optional. Offline, the on-phone model answers alone.

Why it sometimes says "not sure"

An app that always gives an answer is not more useful — it is less trustworthy. If the photo is blurred, too dark, or shows something the model has not seen, Krishi Mitra says it is unsure and asks for a clearer photo. Spraying the wrong medicine costs a farmer money and can damage the crop, so a plain "I do not know" is the honest answer.

Getting a good photo

  • Fill the frame with one leaf, not the whole field.
  • Take it in daylight, but not in harsh midday shadow.
  • Include the damaged part — spots, edges, or discoloured patches.
  • Hold steady; a sharp photo gives a far better answer than a close one that is blurred.
